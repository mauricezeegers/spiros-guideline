# SPIROS 2026 checklist: reporting guideline for observational study protocols

SPIROS (Standardized Protocol Items Recommendations for Observational Studies) is the reporting guideline for protocols of observational studies: cohort, case-control and cross-sectional studies. The SPIROS 2026 Statement gives a checklist of 33 items (55 sub-items) with the minimum content of an observational study protocol; the SPIROS 2026 Explanation and Elaboration paper gives the rationale and model examples for each item.

* Interactive checklist: https://mauricezeegers.github.io/spiros-guideline/
* Repository, authors and how to cite: https://github.com/mauricezeegers/spiros-guideline
* Licence: CC BY 4.0 (examples quoted from published protocols keep the licence of their source)

## 1. Administrative information

### Item 1. Title

* **1** Title: Descriptive title identifying the study design and stating that the document is a study protocol

**Explanation**

In crafting the title of the observational study protocol, it is essential to provide a brief description of the main topic or subject of the study. This provides readers with a clear understanding of the research focus. Additionally, the title should clearly indicate the type of observational study being conducted, such as "cross-sectional study," "cohort study," or "case-control study," and where space allows, also the core study objectives. This helps in accurately categorising and indexing the study protocol in databases. It is required to clearly indicate in the title that the document is a study protocol rather than the final report of the study.

Mentioning the specific population or group being studied, including demographics like age group, gender, or geographic location, and including the directionality, such as "prospective" or "retrospective" for cohort studies, further enhances the title's informativeness.

In essence, a comprehensive title of the observational study protocol should include (a) the study design, (b) the exposure and outcome of interest, (c) the study population and/or (d) setting in which the research will be conducted, and (e) should explicitly state that it is a study protocol. An article by Tullu [[link](https://doi.org/10.4103/sja.SJA_685_18)] provides a checklist for developing effective research titles, which may also be useful for observational study protocols. For journal submission, authors should follow the journal’s specified title word limit. When strict journal word or character limits preclude including all five recommended elements, authors should prioritise elements (a) through (d).

**Examples**

> “Adherence to Mediterranean diet in Italy (ARIANNA) cross-sectional survey: study protocol.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2022-067534)]

> “Social and economic impact of diabetics in Bangladesh: protocol for a case-control study.” [[link](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2458-13-1217)]

> “Outpatient parenteral antimicrobial therapy in Germany: a prospective cohort study protocol.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2022-061417)]

> “Protocol for development and validation of a clinical prediction model for adverse pregnancy outcomes in women with gestational diabetes.” [[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2020-038845)]

> “Effectiveness of Specific Health Check-ups in Japan for the primary prevention of obesity-related diseases: a protocol for a target trial emulation.” [[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2022-070417)]

### Item 2. Protocol version

* **2** Protocol version: Version or amendment number with date and summary of the changes

**Explanation**

Providing protocol version allows researchers and stakeholders to monitor any changes made to the study design or procedures over time. The protocol may be revised in response to ethics committee feedback or to reflect changes required during the conduct of the study. Each version of the protocol serves to document these changes, promoting transparency and accountability in the research process. Sequentially labelling and providing date for each version helps prevent confusion and ensures clarity regarding the most recent document.

Explicitly listing changes relative to the previous version is essential, as it enhances transparency and facilitates study conduct, review, and oversight. Transparent tracking of versions and amendments contributes to the overall integrity of the study, providing assurance that any modifications are documented and accounted for throughout the research process. Revisions made during protocol development and in response to ethics committee (item 25a) feedback can be summarised collectively; the version approved by the ethics committee should be clearly identified and dated. Amendments made after approval should be listed individually, with date and rationale, and previous versions should remain accessible. Researchers can register their protocols in a public repository, such as the Open Science Framework (OSF) protocols.io, bio-protocol, Zenodo, Figshare or other similar platforms, creating a time-stamped record of their research plans. This allows researchers and reviewers to track changes over time, see previous versions, and understand the evolution of their work [[link](https://osf.io/)].

**Examples**

> Hypothetical example: Version Number: XYZ original Version date: 15 January 2026 Version Number: XYZ amendment 01 Version date: 18 March 2026 Primary reason for amendment: Sample size was not adjusted for the dropouts. Summary of revision made: The sample size was Increased by 10% while factoring in a 10% dropout rate.

### Item 3. Protocol summary

* **3** Protocol summary: An informative summary of the study protocol

**Explanation**

The summary for an observational study protocol should briefly outline the (a) study's background, (b) objectives, (c) design, (d) methods including exposure(s), (e) planned sample size, (f) data source (if using pre-existing data), (g) main analysis, (h) outcomes of interest, (i) key limitations and, and (j) anticipated implications. Follow the word limit of the target journal or registry.

For writing the protocol summary of specific research designs, such as clinical prediction or studies using observational data to emulate a target trial, researchers could refer to relevant reporting guidelines, such as TRIPOD+AI [[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-078378)] or TARGET[[link](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2025.13350)]. This would help ensure that the protocol summary appropriately includes all the items required for the abstract of the respective study type. This gives readers a quick understanding of the research without delving into the full protocol document. Avoid the use of non-standard acronyms in the summary. The SPIROS 2026 guideline recommends specifying the protocol registration number (Item 33a) and the date of registration at the end of the protocol summary.

**Examples**

> “Introduction: In rural and difficult-to-access settings, early and accurate recognition of febrile children at risk of progressing to serious illness could contribute to improved patient outcomes and better resource allocation. This study aims to develop a prognostic clinical prediction tool to assist community healthcare providers identify febrile children who might benefit from referral or admission for facility-based medical care. Methods and analysis: This prospective observational study will recruit at least 4900 paediatric inpatients and outpatients under the age of 5 years presenting with an acute febrile illness to seven hospitals in six countries across Asia. A venous blood sample and nasopharyngeal swab is collected from each participant and detailed clinical data recorded at presentation, and each day for the first 48 hours of admission for inpatients. Multianalyte assays are performed at reference laboratories to measure a panel of host biomarkers, as well as targeted aetiological investigations for common bacterial and viral pathogens. Clinical outcome is ascertained on day 2 and day 28. Presenting syndromes, clinical outcomes and aetiology of acute febrile illness will be described and compared across sites. Following the latest guidance in prediction model building, a prognostic clinical prediction model, combining simple clinical features and measurements of host biomarkers, will be derived and geographically externally validated. The performance of the model will be evaluated in specific presenting clinical syndromes and fever aetiologies.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/BMJOPEN-2020-045826)]

### Item 4. Sponsor and funder details

* **4** Sponsor and funder details: Names and roles of the sponsor(s) and funder(s), and types of financial, material (e.g., equipment, drugs) and other (e.g., technical, data access) support

**Explanation**

There is substantial evidence that studies sponsored by entities with competing interests, particularly industry sponsors, often exhibit certain biases [[link](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0039-1700834),[link](https://doi.org/10.1097/DCR.0000000000001298),[link](https://doi.org/10.1111/andr.13064),[link](https://doi.org/10.2105/AJPH.2018.304677)]. These biases can affect various aspects of the study. Providing the name and contact details of the sponsor and funder in any research protocol, including an observational research protocol is crucial for transparency and accountability. It helps disclose potential conflicts of interest (CoI) (item 5), ensures adherence to ethical standards, and aids in regulatory compliance. Overall, it raises trust among stakeholders and upholds the integrity of the research.

The sponsor of a study is typically an organisation or institution that takes primary responsibility for the initiation, management, and funding of the research project. They often oversee the study's design, implementation, and reporting. Sponsors can be a person, academic institutions, government agencies, non-profit organisations, or commercial entities. On the other hand, a funder of the study is an entity that provides financial support for the research but may not necessarily have direct involvement in the study's management or decision-making processes. Funders can include the sponsor itself, as well as other organisations or individuals who contribute financially to the research.

The protocol should state the sponsor’s and funder’s role in study design, data access, analysis, interpretation and the decision to publish, and who has final authority over each. In addition, the protocol must explicitly identify the people or organisation(s) sponsoring and funding the study, providing relevant details and affiliations. It should explain the financial or non-financial support obtained from the sponsor and/or funder, including grants, and any material support offered by the sponsor and/or funder, which may include essential supplies, equipment, medications, or other resources crucial for study execution. The protocol should also address any other forms of support received, such as technical assistance, expertise, or access to data or facilities. Item 4 identifies sponsors and funders and their roles, Item 32 lists funding sources and grant details, and Item 5 covers investigators’ competing interests; cross-referencing between these items is acceptable.

**Examples**

> “This work is supported by MSF-OCBA and the UK Wellcome Trust (219644/Z/19/Z) and cosponsored by the University of Oxford and MSF-OCBA. MSF-OCBA maintains a cosponsor and investigator role within the study.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/BMJOPEN-2020-045826)]

### Item 5. Conflict of interest statements

* **5** Conflict of interest statements: Statement of any financial and other competing interests of each investigator

**Explanation**

There are many potential sources of competing interests. This includes financial competing interest, academic, idealistic, or personal competing interests (also known as non-financial competing interests) [[link](https://doi.org/10.2519/JOSPT.2020.0103)]. Disclosure of financial associations of authors of articles published in biomedical journals has become common practice [[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/BMJ.B4144)]. The International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) provide templates for drafting CoI statements [[link](https://icmje.org/recommendations/browse/roles-and-responsibilities/author-responsibilities--conflicts-of-interest.html)]. SPIROS 2026 encourages investigators to clearly outline any potential CoI in observational studies at the protocol stage.

CoI in research extends to all stakeholders including principal investigators, sponsor, funder, ethics committees, and the institutions. Overall, including a CoI statement in observational study protocols is essential for maintaining transparency, ethical integrity, and credibility in research. It allows stakeholders and ethics review boards (ERB) or Institutional ethics committees (IRB) (Item 25a) to assess potential biases, make informed decisions, and uphold the highest standards of scientific integrity [[link](https://doi.org/10.4103/2229-3485.106375)]. If there are no competing interests, this should be stated. Declarations should be updated when the protocol is amended and at publication, and the protocol should describe how disclosed interests will be managed.

**Examples**

> “The authors have no conflict of interest to report” [[link](https://doi.org/10.1097/NNR.0000000000000657)]

> “ACB has received grants from Fujirebio. JEH is a trustee of the Avon Mesothelioma Foundation. KGB has received grants from Rocket Medical. NAM has received grants from Becton Dickinson & Rocket Medical and is a member of the advisory board for Becton Dickinson. No members of the project team received any financial incentives for their contribution.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2022-060850)]

> “The author declares a potential conflict of interest. Dominik Liebetrau is a consultant for Medtronic GmbH, Earl-Bakken-Platz 1 40670 Meerbusch, Germany. Dominik Liebetrau declares to have received honorary fees from plus medica GmbH & Co, Willstätterstraße 13, 40549 Düsseldorf for professional presentations. Jörg Teßarek declares to have received honorary fees from plus medica GmbH & Co, Willstätterstraße 13, 40549 Düsseldorf for professional presentations. All other authors have declared no potential conflicts of interest with respect to this research, authorship, and/or publication of this article.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1186/s42155-023-00404-8)]

> “Non-financial interests: Author A is on the board of directors of Y and receives no compensation as member of the board of directors.”

### Item 6. Investigators

* **6a** Investigators’ names: Names of the principal and co-investigators
* **6b** Affiliation of investigators: Affiliated institutions of each investigator at the time of protocol submission
* **6c** Contact details: E-mail address (preferably institutional) of the principal investigator(s) and the corresponding author

**Explanation**

People who have significantly contributed to the development of an observational study protocol or who will be core team members conducting the research should include their names, affiliations, and contact details, regardless of whether the protocol is intended for publication. If the author has more than one affiliation, SPIROS 2026 recommends listing only those affiliations that reflect their appointment at the time of the study and are substantively connected to the research. Affiliations held solely for administrative or funding reasons should not be listed here; any relevant funding or institutional relationship should instead be disclosed under Item 5 (Conflict of interest statements).

Including investigator names and affiliations in an observational research protocol enhances transparency, credibility, and accountability in the research process. It helps establish the expertise of the research team, facilitates communication, and ensures adherence to ethical and regulatory standards. Providing these details also help in identifying potential CoI (Item 5) and ghost or honorary authorship (item 29c). Where required by journal or institutional policy, the protocol may also include a signature page featuring the signatures and signing-off dates of all researchers involved in the study, to formally confirm approval and accountability prior to submission; this reflects current practice within some research teams but is not yet a universal requirement. SPIROS 2026 also encourage to provide the Open Researcher and Contributor ID (ORCID) of the investigators who contributed to the protocol [[link](https://doi.org/10.1080/02763869.2016.1152139)].

**Examples**

> “Authors Name A Chandna ,1,2 E M Aderie 3 Author affiliations 1Angkor Hospital for Children, Cambodia Oxford Medical Research Unit, Siem Reap, Cambodia 2Centre for Tropical Medicine and Global Health, University of Oxford, Oxford, UK 3Médecins Sans Frontières Operational Centre Barcelona, Barcelona, Spain ORCID iDs” A Chandna http:// orcid. org/ 0000- 0003- 1313- 7922 Vu Quoc Dat http:// orcid. org/ 0000- 0002- 5904- 5970 ” [[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/BMJOPEN-2020-045826)]

## 2. Study design and setting

### Item 7. Background, review of prior research, rationale

* **7a** Background of the study: The research question and its scientific and clinical or public health context
* **7b** Review of prior research: Summary of relevant existing research (published or unpublished)
* **7c** Rationale of study: Justification for conducting the study and for the chosen design

**Explanation**

Providing a clear description of the research question and the scientific background of the study contextualises the research within the existing body of knowledge. This section outlines what is already known about the topic and identifies gaps in current understanding, thus establishing the rationale for conducting the study.

A summary of relevant existing research, whether published or unpublished, or with positive or negative findings is essential for several reasons. It helps researchers avoid duplication of efforts by identifying what has already been studied. Additionally, it allows them to build upon existing knowledge and understand any conflicting or inconsistent findings. Including unpublished research can also mitigate publication bias and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the topic. it is strongly recommended that an up-to-date systematic review of relevant studies be summarised and cited in the protocol.

Justifying why the study is being conducted is critical for ethical and scientific reasons. This section should outline the significance of the research question, including its potential impact on clinical practice, public health, or scientific knowledge. It should also explain why the chosen observational study design is appropriate for addressing the research question and how the study findings may contribute to filling gaps in knowledge or informing future research or policy decisions.

Overall, including these elements in the protocol ensures transparency, and ethical conduct in observational research, while also helping to maximise the study's scientific value and impact. This information is also vital for funders, research ethics committees, ERBs, IRBs, and other stakeholders involved in evaluating the scientific and ethical integrity of the study.

Therefore, the protocol should include the background of study including a clear explanation of the main research question along with a summary of existing evidence, a detailed justification for the study and discussion of its feasibility.

**Examples**

> “Background: Tuberculosis (TB) is a major health hazard and diagnosis is especially challenging in resource-limited settings (RLS). Chest x-ray (CXR) is a useful tool in the diagnosis and monitoring of pulmonary M. tuberculosis infection and forms part of diagnostic protocols and management guidelines from both WHO and MSF. However, studies have shown that error rates due to under or over-reading CXRs can be as high as 20%. As the characteristics of TB on CXRs are non-specific, CXRs are therefore prone to high subjectivity with regards to interpretation and show high inter-observer variability in non-radiologists and other nonexperts. CXRs are therefore inaccurate as an exclusive diagnostic tool for the diagnosis of TB. The ability of a CXR to be suggestive of pulmonary TB infection depends on the presentation of the disease, which in turn is influenced by a range of other factors such as HIV status and sex of the patient, as well as the technical quality of the radiograph. Diagnosing TB in HIV positive patients is particularly challenging due to higher rates of smear-negative results. No single or combination of clinical or radiographic criteria has been shown to reliably diagnose smear-negative pulmonary TB among HIV-infected patients with unexplained cough in a low-income setting. The CXR is therefore an important contribution to the combined criteria for diagnosing TB in such a patient group and is endorsed by the WHO for use in HIV infected patients…. “ Summary of relevant existing research: “According to data from four African studies, the sensitivity of CXRs for TB screening in HIV positive patients can range from 27% to 81%. CXRs have the advantage of immediately available results in contrast to TB culture which can take weeks before a result is known. This is a crucial advantage for HIV/TB co-infected patients where treatment should not be delayed. …One study on x-ray classification in which 1,100 films were read by 90 experienced physicians and radiologists found up to 34 % disagreement on the question: "is the film normal?" and a 28 % disagreement on the question: "Is there a cavity present?". High reader variability in interpreting CXRs directly correlates with low diagnostic performance. When a standardised set of CXRs was interpreted by 162 radiologists and residents, the group with the highest diagnostic accuracy also showed the smallest inter-reader variability. results imply that non-radiologists would be able to diagnose more accurately if they were able to improve their ability to interpret CXRs more accurately.” Rationale of study: “The reason for conducting this study is to improve the interpretation accuracy of CXRs used in the diagnosis of TB infection by non-specialists. In MSF settings, the interpretation of CXRs is usually not done by radiologists or specialists but rather by a medical officer or general physician, which is a group that is less trained in the interpretation of CXRs in comparison to a radiologist. In order to improve the diagnosis of TB, approaches to improve CXR interpretation is an important factor to consider. The utilisation of a tick-sheet has the possibility to reduce variability and improve diagnostic accuracy through standardisation and is therefore a potentially useful tool in improving TB diagnostics. The reason for conducting this study is to determine whether reading a CXR using a tick-sheet and a short four hour training session, improves clinicians CXR interpretation accuracy compared to an expert reference standard, whether it reduces inter-reader variability, and improves the accuracy of a diagnosis of active TB.” [[link](https://scienceportal.msf.org/assets/6970)]

### Item 8. Aim, objectives and intention

* **8a** Aim: Broader aims of the study
* **8b** Objective/s of the study: Primary and secondary objectives, including any prespecified hypotheses
* **8c** Type of research question: Specify whether the study aims to (a) describe (e.g., prevalence, incidence, patterns), (b) estimate the causal effect of an exposure, or (c) predict outcomes

**Explanation**

Providing broader aims, primary and secondary objectives help to clarify the main purpose of the study. It outlines the scope of the research and helps stakeholders understand the intended outcomes or goals [[link](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Thomas%20DR%2C%20Hodges%20ID.%20Designing%20and%20managing%20your%20research%20project%3A%20Core%20skills%20for%20social%20and%20health%20research.%20SAGE%20Pub)].

Well-written objectives (Primary and secondary objectives) in the protocol provide clear and specific goals for the study, including the populations, exposures, outcomes, and parameters to be estimated. The primary objective represents the central question the study seeks to answer. It typically guides the overall study design and statistical planning, including sample size determination. Some studies include secondary objectives that explore additional outcomes, which may or may not be linked to the primary objective. The clear objectives help researchers stay focused during the study design, data collection, and analysis phases, ensuring that the research remains aligned with its intended purpose. Furthermore, well-defined objectives guide the selection of appropriate study design, statistical methods, and analysis techniques. This help researchers determine which variables to measure, how to measure them, and how to interpret the results in relation to the research questions or hypotheses. To ensure your research objectives are clearly defined and attainable, apply the SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound) criteria [[link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.evalprogplan.2016.12.009)].

Descriptive studies estimate frequencies or associations without a causal interpretation. Causal (aetiological) studies estimate the effect of an exposure on an outcome and should define the target estimand (population, exposure contrast, outcome, time frame and effect measure) and how confounding will be addressed. Prediction studies estimate the probability of current or future outcomes and should follow TRIPOD+AI [[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-078378)]. Diagnostic accuracy studies are outside the scope of SPIROS 2026 (see STARD) [[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2016-012799)].

As new insights emerge during data analysis, researchers may identify important subgroups or additional analyses that were not originally planned. Describing these deviations from the original objectives in the study report ensures transparency and helps readers interpret the results accurately.

If applicable, the investigators should also specify hypotheses that the study aims to address. These hypotheses may be just description of population or exploring potential associations between multiple exposures and outcomes or predict outcome.

Hence, the protocol must include the (a) aim of the study, (b) clearly defined primary and secondary objectives (if applicable) with measurable endpoints, and, if relevant, hypotheses to be tested.

**Examples**

> “Aim: To investigate factors associated with partner violence. Objectives: Objective 1: To examine whether alcohol consumption is associated with increased partner violence. Objective 2: To examine whether labour force status (employment, unemployment, not in the labour force) is associated with variations in the incidence of partner violence. Objective 3: To explore differences between couples with an extended history of partner violence and couples with only a brief, recent history of partner violence. Hypothesis: Higher consumption of alcohol will be associated with more incidents of partner violence.” [[link](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Thomas%20DR%2C%20Hodges%20ID.%20Designing%20and%20managing%20your%20research%20project%3A%20Core%20skills%20for%20social%20and%20health%20research.%20SAGE%20Pub)]

### Item 9. Study design, data sources and setting

* **9a** Study design and data sources: Description of study design (case control, cross-sectional or cohort) and time dimension of the study (Retrospective or prospective). For studies using pre-existing data, provide a description of the data source (e.g., electronic health records, patient registries, administrative claims, etc.)
* **9b** Study setting: Description of the study setting (e.g., community-based, hospital-based) and details of precise locations of the study sites

**Explanation**

Observational studies include various study designs, each tailored to address specific research questions and hypotheses [[link](https://doi.org/10.1592/phco.30.10.973),[link](https://doi.org/10.11613/BM.2014.022),[link](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Bouter%20LM%2C%20Zeegers%20MP%2C%20Li%20T%20%28Writer%20on%20evidence-based%20medicine%29.%20Textbook%20of%20epidemiology.%202nd%20Edition.%202023.)]. The most common observational study designs include cohort study, case-control Study, cross-Sectional Study, ecological Study, case Series/Case Report, nested case-control study, and case-crossover study.

Each observational study design has its strengths and limitations, and the choice of design depends on factors such as the research question, available resources, and ethical considerations.

The SPIROS 2026 guideline primarily focuses on three main observational study designs: i.e., cohort study, case-control study, and cross-sectional Study. Nevertheless, its principles may also be relevant and applicable to other study designs within the realm of observational studies.

Clarifying and justifying the selected study design in protocol is important due to its direct relationship with the study objectives and its significant impact on various facets of the study, including methodology, implementation, financial considerations, outcomes, and the interpretation of findings. The protocol should also state when exposure and outcome data are, or were, recorded relative to the start of the study.

Many observational studies utilise preexisting real-world data. Real-world data are defined as data not originally collected for research purposes but generated during routine clinical practice. These data sources include electronic health records, administrative and claims databases, patient registries, and patient-reported outcomes. The protocol of an observational study should explicitly specify the data sources. Additionally, in line with the RECORD reporting guidelines [[link](https://doi.org/10.1371/JOURNAL.PMED.1001885)], studies involving person-level, institutional-level, or other types of data linkage across two or more databases should describe the linkage methods that will be used and the procedures that will be followed to assess the quality and accuracy of the linkage.

Describing the study setting (e.g., community-based, hospital-based) helps readers understand the environment in which the study is conducted. This context provides important background information that may influence the interpretation of study findings. The characteristics of the study setting can impact the generalisability of study findings. Providing details of the precise locations of the study sites enhances the transparency of the study methodology. Readers can evaluate whether the chosen sites are representative of the target population and assess the potential for selection bias. Clear documentation of the study setting, and site locations facilitates the reproducibility of the study. Other researchers who wish to replicate or build upon the study can use this information to identify suitable study sites and compare their findings. In some cases, the specific location of study sites may have ethical implications, such as ensuring participant confidentiality and privacy (Item 27c). Including this information allows reviewers and stakeholders to assess whether appropriate ethical safeguards are in place. Detailed information about study site locations is essential for logistical planning, including participant recruitment, data collection, and coordination with local authorities or institutions. This information helps ensure smooth implementation of the study.

Therefore, the countries, State, city/village names, type of setting (e.g., urban or rural), sources (e.g., paediatric hospital, outpatient clinic, or nutrition rehabilitation centre) and the likely number of planned study sites should be reported in the observational study protocols. However, report locations at a level of detail that does not compromise participant anonymity, particularly for small communities (Item 27c).

**Examples**

> Item 9a: “An individually matched hospital-based case–control design will be used in this study. All participants will be selected from individuals admitted to the psychiatric ward for medical management, but only patients with FEP will be included for further evaluation. According to the recent consensus on red flags of suspected AIP, participants with at least one sign will be categorized into the case group, while all other patients will be enrolled as controls..” [[link](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2022.976159)]

> Item 9a: “We will use a population-based retrospective cohort study to identify potential patient, provider and institution-level factors and continuity of care factors independently associated with OAC-related adverse events in seniors using routinely collected administrative health data.” [[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2019-036537)]

> Data source: “We will use electronic health record (EHR) data from the Accelerating Data Value Across a National Community Health Center Network clinical data research network, which has data from community health centres in 22 states, some that did and some that did not expand Medicaid.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/BMJOPEN-2018-025975)]

> Item 9b: “The study will be conducted in two public, primary care, outpatient clinics located in the municipality of Pedro Aguirre Cerda in the southern district of Santiago, the capital of Chile. We expect to begin recruitment in the second quarter of 2022 and conclude after 6 months. Both outpatient clinics provide care for the population within the family health model framework as per ministerial technical guidance. Pedro Aguirre Cerda municipality has a population of 101,174 according to the most recent national census (2017), with an average age of 38.3 years. According to the National Household Survey, in 2020, 11.7% of this municipality’s population was classified as poor by income and the 2020 Social Priority Index rated it as having a medium-high social priority.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2022-061345)]

## 3. Population and eligibility

### Item 11. Sample size

* **11** Sample size: Planned sample size and its justification, including a sample size or precision calculation with its assumptions, where applicable

**Explanation**

Specifying the sample size in the protocol of observational studies is essential for ensuring the scientific validity, reliability, generalisability, cost-effectiveness, and ethical conduct of the research. It is a critical component of study planning and design, influencing various aspects of the research process and the interpretation of study findings.

The sample size of a study is important for obtaining an outcome estimate with an adequately narrow confidence interval, allowing meaningful answers to research questions. Small studies give imprecise estimates, and they are more likely to be published when their results are statistically significant, which can lead to overestimated effects [[link](https://doi.org/10.1183/09031936.00136408)].

In observational studies, the importance of sample size determination varies depending on the context. If analysing existing data, the focus is on whether the analysis will yield results with sufficient statistical precision to add substantially to the existing evidence base.

Formal sample size calculations may be useful when planning prospective observational studies, although it involves uncertainty due to imprecise estimates of event rates and other assumptions. Moreover, precision in the final analysis may be affected by confounding variables, measurement precision, and exclusions.

SPIROS 2026 encourage investigators to report relevant sample size calculations in the protocol if performed. Otherwise, they should outline the considerations that will determine the study sample size (e.g., all patients admitted in hospital between January 2025 to July 2025 will be included). Although observational studies typically involve minimal risk, in studies involving invasive procedures or significant participant burden, SPIROS 2026 recommends that investigators consider specifying not only a minimum sample size but also a feasible range (e.g., minimum and maximum limits).

Sample size estimation for observational studies is nicely explained in an article by Wang and Ji [[link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chest.2020.03.010)] and in an article by Charan and Biswas [[link](https://doi.org/10.4103/0253-7176.116232)]. The web-based, open-source tools, such as OpenEpi [[link](https://doi.org/10.1177/003335490912400320)], StatCalc: Statistical Calculators, [[link](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Centers%20for%20Disease%20Control%20and%20Prevention.%20StatCalc%3A%20Statistical%20Calculators.%20Epi%20InfoTM%20User%20Guide.%202017.)] online sample size calculator (http://riskcalc.org:3838/samplesize/) developed by Wang and Ji [[link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chest.2020.03.010),[link](https://riskcalc.org/samplesize/)] and other statistical software like “STATA” and ‘R’ can help students and researchers in calculating the sample size for observational studies [[link](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Kar%20SS%2C%20Ramalingam%20A.%20Is%2030%20The%20Magic%20Number%3F%20Issues%20In%20Sample%20Size%20Estimation.%20National%20Journal%20of%20Community%20Medicine.%20)].

It is important to provide estimates or assumptions for key parameters needed for the sample size calculation. For cross-sectional studies investigator should include the expected prevalence or proportion of the outcome in the population, the desired level of precision (e.g., margin of error), and the desired level of confidence (typically 95%).

In the cohort study it is important to provide estimates or assumptions for the expected incidence or prevalence of the outcome of interest in the population over the study period. Estimate the effect size (e.g., risk ratio, hazard ratio) that the study aims to detect. This reflects the minimum clinically or scientifically meaningful difference between exposed and unexposed groups. Specify the desired statistical power (typically 80% or higher), significance level (usually 0.05) for detecting the specified effect size and indicate whether a one- or two-sided test will be used, as the choice affects the required sample size [[link](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0895-4356(00)00276-6)]. Define the duration of follow-up. This influences the number of events expected to occur and thus affects the required sample size.

For case control studies provide estimates or assumptions for the expected prevalence of exposure among cases and controls in the population. Estimate the effect size (e.g., odds ratio) that the study aims to detect. This reflects the minimum clinically or scientifically meaningful difference in exposure prevalence between cases and controls. Specify the desired statistical and significance level for detecting the specified effect size. Determine the desired ratio of cases to controls in the study. This may vary depending on the research question and study design.

For sample size calculation for any observational study design consider potential loss to follow-up rates, non-response rates and withdrawal rates when determining the required sample size. This ensures that the sample size calculation accounts for participants who may drop out or lost to follow-up during the data collection or follow-up period (item 19a).

Account for any confounding factors (item 17) that may need to be adjusted for in the analysis. This may require a larger sample size to ensure adequate power to detect associations after controlling for confounding.

For studies using existing data, a formal power calculation is usually not needed; report the expected number of participants and events and the precision this is expected to give. Post hoc power calculations are not recommended.

SPIROS 2026 recommends that the observational study protocol should include the precision or power calculation method used to estimate the necessary sample size based on the primary outcome. The protocol should outline any statistical assumptions made regarding distribution and estimates of the difference to be detected, substantiated by appropriate justification. Additionally, the chosen levels of significance, type of test (one-sided or two-sided), and power should be specified. Furthermore, it is advisable to provide a reference or detailed explanation of the method or formula used for the calculation to ensure transparency and reproducibility in the research process.

**Examples**

> “The power calculation was based on the increased coefficient of determination (R2) from a baseline simple linear model to a multivariate model that regresses body fat percentage on potential predictors (ie, alternate height, BMI, race, and age). Pilot studies from the literature suggest that the correlation between the body fat percentage measured using DLW and alternate height (arm span and recumbent length) is in the range of approximately 0.6 to 0.7 (R2=0.36-0.49). As we will use 10-fold cross-validation to construct our predictive models for 2 gender groups, a sample size of 94 (232/2 × 0.9 × 0.9) achieves 90% power to detect an increase of R2≥0.066 (0.078) attributed to 1 (2) independent variable(s) using an F test with a significance level of .05. The variables tested are adjusted for 1 independent variable in the simple linear model with an R2 of 0.36. We assume that 10% of the participants will not complete all measures based on pilot data. Because it is likely that we will include >2 additional variables in the final model, the proposed sample size will have sufficient power to detect a clinically meaningful increase in R2.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.2196/52779)]

> “We hypothesized that the mean bisphenol A levels in normal-weight children would be 2.8 ng/ml (a cut-off level in the US children for 2nd quartile bisphenol A levels) and 5.4 ng/ml (a cut off level for 4th quartile in US children) for the present study. Their corresponding SD values of 6 and 8 were taken from Indian data. Considering an alpha level of 5% and power of 80%, we calculated the required sample size of 93 children (rounding to 100) in each group will be recruited, giving us a total sample size of 300 children.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.5114/pedm.2022.112857)]

### Item 12. Sampling procedure

* **12** Sampling procedure: Detailed description of the sampling frame and sampling strategy (e.g., simple random, stratified, cluster, systematic, consecutive, convenience, purposive)

**Explanation**

Reporting the detailed description of the sampling frame and sampling strategy in observational study protocols involves providing clear information about how the study population will be selected.

Investigators should describe the sampling frame, which is the list or source from which the study participants will be drawn. Provide details about the population or group from which the sample will be selected. This may include geographic location, specific demographic criteria, or other defining characteristics.

It is crucial to explain the method by which participants will be selected from the sampling frame. Common probability sampling strategies include: (a) Simple random sampling: each member of the sampling frame has an equal chance of being selected; (b) Stratified random sampling: the sampling frame is divided into strata based on certain characteristics, and participants are randomly selected from each stratum; (c) Cluster sampling: the sampling frame is divided into clusters (e.g., geographic areas, schools), and entire clusters are randomly selected; and (d) Systematic sampling: participants are selected at regular intervals from the sampling frame. Common non-probability sampling strategies include: (e) Consecutive sampling: all eligible individuals are included as they present (e.g., at a clinic) during a defined period, which is common in hospital-based studies; (f) Convenience sampling: participants are selected based on their availability or accessibility; (g) Purposive sampling: participants are selected deliberately because they have characteristics relevant to the research question; (h) Quota sampling: participants are recruited until a predefined number is reached in each subgroup; and (i) Snowball or respondent-driven sampling: participants recruit other eligible individuals from their networks, which is useful for hard-to-reach populations. Time-location sampling, as in the example above, combines elements of both: venues and time slots where the target population gathers are listed and sampled, and individuals present during the selected slots are invited. For complex sampling designs, describe the sampling weights and design effects that will be accounted for in the analysis (Item 18). Sampling methods and how to choose a sampling technique for research is well described in an article by Hamed Taherdoost [[link](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.3205035)]. Investigator should provide rationale for the chosen sampling strategy in an observational study protocol, considering factors such as the study objectives, feasibility, representativeness of the sample, and resources available.

**Examples**

> “In this community-based cross-sectional study, self-identified transgender and non-binary (TGNB) people will be recruited through a two-stage time-location sampling approach. First, community settings in which TGNB people gather will be mapped to develop an accurate sampling frame. Secondly, a multistage sampling design is applied involving a stratification based on setting type (healthcare facilities vs outreach events), a selection of clusters by systematic sampling and a simple random selection of TGNB people within each cluster."[[link](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0266078)]

### Item 13. Participant selection

* **13** Participant selection: Eligibility (inclusion and exclusion) criteria and the source and methods of participant selection, specified by study design:

Cohort studies: Description of inclusion and exclusion criteria, and the source and methods of participant selection (exposed and unexposed). For matched cohort studies, give matching criteria and number of exposed and unexposed.

Case-control study: Description of inclusion and exclusion criteria, and the source and methods of case ascertainment and control selection. Give the rationale for the choice of cases and controls. Give diagnostic criteria for identifying cases (if applicable). For matched case-control studies, give matching criteria and the number of controls per case.

Cross-sectional study: Description of the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and the source and methods of participant selection.

**Explanation**

Describing the inclusion and exclusion criteria, as well as the source and methods of participant selection, is important in an observational study protocol. Clearly defining inclusion and exclusion criteria enable assessing if the study population is representative of the target population. This enhances the internal validity of the study by reducing the potential for selection bias.

By clearly defining inclusion and exclusion criteria for exposure and outcome variables in cohort studies, and for cases and controls in case-control studies, researchers can minimise confounding factors that may interfere with the relationship between exposure and outcome. Exclusion criteria can also help exclude individuals with known confounders or other factors that may influence the study results. In registry-based observational studies, inclusion and exclusion criteria should specify how eligible participants will be identified within the registry. e.g., based on diagnostic codes, procedure codes, laboratory values, or other recorded variables.

Transparently describing the source and methods of participant selection – such as exposed and unexposed participants in cohort studies, cases and controls in the case-control studies, and participants from a sampling frame in cross-sectional studies - in protocols allows other researchers to understand how the study population will be selected. This enhances the reproducibility of the study, minimise selection bias, and allows for comparison with other studies.

In case control-studies, if applicable, providing diagnostic criteria for identifying cases helps ensure consistency in case identification across different study sites or investigators. This improves the reliability and validity of the study findings.

Matching in cohort and case-control studies can potentially boost statistical power, reducing the required sample size for analysis [[link](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Evans%20S.%20Commentary%3A%20Matched%20cohorts%20can%20be%20useful.%20Br%20Med%20J.%202003%3B326.),[link](https://doi.org/10.37737/ace.22005)]. While it may appear that matching ensures similarity between exposed and unexposed groups, or cases and control groups in terms of background variables, it does not guarantee complete adjustment for confounding or other sources of error [[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.i969)]. Advanced techniques like propensity score analysis can be advantageous in addressing these limitations [[link](https://doi.org/10.1002/pds.3263)]. If propensity score matching will be used, the protocol must explain why it is needed, specify which baseline variables will be included in the propensity score model, state the model type that will be used to estimate the scores (e.g., logistic regression, probit, or machine learning), and describe how subjects will be matched or grouped (e.g., nearest-neighbour or caliper matching).

In matched cohort or case -control studies, providing details about the matching criteria (e.g., age, sex, comorbidities) and the number of exposed and unexposed individuals or number of controls per case in each matched set is important. This information helps readers understand the rationale for matching and ensures that the exposed and unexposed or cases and controls groups are balanced with respect to potential confounders [[link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chest.2020.03.010),[link](https://doi.org/10.4103/0253-7176.116232)].

**Examples**

> Participant selection in a cohort study: “All patients of the Department of Psychiatry and Psychotherapy III at the University Hospital Ulm, Germany, whose disorder is classified as schizophrenia spectrum on hospital admission according to the current version of DSM-5 are potentially eligible for inclusion. In the course of data evaluation, a distinction is then made between whether the diagnosis is an initial one or whether the patient already suffers from a schizophreniform disorder. The control group is recruited from healthy volunteers who are matched for sex and age. The following inclusion and exclusion criteria apply to patients with schizophrenia. Exclusion criteria Age <18 years. Cognitive impairment that makes study participation impossible. Severe visual and hearing impairments. Patients who are unable to give informed consent. Insufficient German language skills, making communication impossible. Inclusion and exclusion criteria for the matched group of healthy subjects are defined as follows: Age ≥18 years. Anamnestic exclusion of a previous psychiatric disorder. Verbal and written volunteer informed consent before the start of measurements. The exclusion criteria are the same as those for patients with schizophrenia.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2021-050501)]

> Participant selection in case-control study: “Definition and selection of cases All male or female patients of any age presenting to a study site with confirmed malaria. Inclusion criteria: Positive microscopy for Plasmodium infection; documented fever or history of fever in the past 48 h; primarily resided in the study area in the previous 3 weeks; residence can be located and mapped; appropriate informed consent obtained. Cases will be classified according to PCR confirmation of Plasmodium spp monoinfection. If the selected individual is not present when the fieldworkers visit the household, then they will return up to three times, which may include an arranged later time on the same day or on subsequent days. Failing this an alternative household will be selected using the same method. Control exclusion criteria: Previously recruited as a case; previously recruited as a control; member of a previously selected control household; age<4 weeks.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2014-006004)]

> Participants selection in cross-sectional study: “Survey population selection criteria: The survey will involve participants aged 15 years and above as adults and less than 15 years as children. ## 4. Exposure and outcome definitions

### Item 14. Study variables and data definitions/measurement

* **14a** Study variables: List all outcomes (primary, secondary and other), exposures, potential confounders and effect modifiers, with their role in the analysis and how confounders were selected.
* **14b** Data definitions/measurement: For each key variable, give its definition, data source, measurement method, timing and coding (for existing data, the code lists or algorithms used). For large databases, a domain-level summary with reference to a data dictionary is sufficient for variables other than exposures, outcomes and confounders.

**Explanation**

The study protocol should provide a comprehensive listing of all the main variables to be collected, whether they serve as an outcome, explanatory factor, potential confounder (item 17), or prespecified falsification endpoints (negative control outcomes, i.e., outcomes not expected to be affected by the exposure). For outcome variables, protocol should provide definition and description of all primary, secondary and other outcomes or end points, including diagnostic criteria.

Additionally, the source from which the data will be derived must be delineated, whether it be questionnaires, electronic health records, or other specific procedures. When relevant, the timing of data collection, whether at baseline, during treatment, or at follow-up intervals, needs to be explicitly stated to ensure accurate temporal tracking. Furthermore, define who is responsible for data collection, be it study personnel or healthcare providers. If standardised tools or procedures are used their details (including the exact name and version of the instrument, the instructions used, and proper citations to prior validation studies that establish their reliability and validity in similar populations) must be provided, while the reliability and validity of non-standard tools should be justified (Item 15a). For studies assessing change over time or evaluating dynamic clinical states, researchers should also report responsiveness (the ability of an instrument to detect clinically meaningful change) whenever known. Similarly, for novel or non-standard tools, addressing feasibility, such as respondent burden, completion time, and administrative complexity, helps justify their practical suitability for the study setting and population. The format of the data to be collected, whether binary, categorical, continuous, or time-to-event, must also be specified. Finally, include data collection forms, data dictionary, code books, and questionnaires as appendices to the protocol ensures accessibility and transparency in data collection procedures. Data dictionaries and codebooks are sometimes used interchangeably but have distinct roles. Codebooks explain the data, while data dictionaries cover both the data and its file structure, offering a more comprehensive overview that may include a codebook [[link](https://doi.org/10.1177/2515245920928007)]. The OSF provide useful resources to create a data dictionary [[link](https://doi.org/10.1177/2515245920928007),[link](https://help.osf.io/article/217-how-to-make-a-data-dictionary)].

Defining and describing primary, secondary, and other outcomes in protocols of studies involving follow-up of participants (e.g. prospective cohort studies) ensures clarity, consistency, and relevance in the research objectives and methodology. It helps researchers and stakeholders understand the key outcomes/endpoints of interest and guides the design, conduct, data collection, analysis, and interpretation of the study. The definition and description of the outcomes should be clear, specific, and relevant to the study aim and objectives. The outcomes should follow the SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound) criteria [[link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.evalprogplan.2016.12.009)] . Additionally, researchers should clearly specify the five core elements of a defined outcome i.e. outcome domain (e.g., cardiovascular mortality), the measurement variable (e.g., fatal myocardial infarction), the specific metric (e.g., hazard ratio or risk difference), the method of aggregation (e.g., time-to-event or cumulative incidence), and the exact timepoint of assessment.

The primary outcome is the main endpoint of interest that directly addresses the research question or hypothesis. Secondary outcomes are additional endpoints that provide supplementary information relevant to the study. Other outcomes encompass any additional measures or variables that are of interest to the study but do not fit neatly into the categories of primary or secondary outcomes. These may include exploratory analyses and subgroup analyses. While not always predefined or prioritised like primary and secondary outcomes, other outcomes should still be clearly described and justified in the protocol to ensure transparency and consistency in data collection and reporting. For exposures, specify timing, dose or intensity, duration, any induction or latency period, how time-varying exposure will be handled, and the reference category.

For studies using large databases, such as electronic health records or administrative claims data with hundreds or thousands of variables, listing every variable is not practical or useful. Instead, authors should provide a general overview of the data collected (e.g., demographics, diagnoses, prescriptions, and laboratory values) and refer readers to data dictionaries or supplementary materials for the complete list of variables. Researchers can also refer to the RECORD reporting guideline [[link](https://doi.org/10.1371/JOURNAL.PMED.1001885)] for important reporting requirements. Importantly, rather than listing every raw data field, protocols and reports should clearly describe the coding algorithms, phenotyping rules, and validation methods used to define eligibility criteria, exposures, outcomes, and key confounders. This should include details such as the specific ICD codes or medication-mapping methods used.

**Examples**

> Defining primary and secondary endpoints of the study: “Primary and secondary endpoints The study primary endpoint is to assess the Deternia Self-Gripping Resorbable Mesh performance in suture line reinforcement after elective midline laparotomy at 12 month postoperatively and demonstrate its efficacy in preventing IH occurrence. IH development will be evaluated both clinically and by imaging with ultrasonography (in case of discrepancy, imagery will be decisive). If there is a suspicion of hernia based on the clinical evaluation, but the ultrasound examination is negative, computed tomography scan might be performed according to site standard of care. IH is clinically defined as any abdominal wall gap with or without a bulge in the area of a postoperative scar perceptible or palpable by clinical examination when a Valsalva maneuver was carried out in the supine decubitus position and/or in the standing position. IH is defined, according to ultrasound examination of the midline laparotomy, as a visible gap within the abdominal wall and/or tissue moving through the abdominal wall by Valsalva maneuver. Secondary endpoints are designed to assess device clinical safety and performance within 36 months postoperatively and include: Incidence of IH at 24 and 36 month follow-up, assessed by imaging and clinical examination (in case of discrepancy imagery will be decisive). Incidence of clinical IH (physical exam) at 3, 6, 12, 24, and 36 month follow-up. Time to IH (from surgery time-point). Time to other adverse device effects (Ade) occurrences (from skin incision time-point). Incidence of all Ades (mesh and mesh-augmented reinforcement procedure) intraoperatively, at discharge, within 3, 6, 12, 24, and 36 month following the use of Deternia Self-Gripping Resorbable Mesh. Incidence of AEs of interest: symptomatic seroma requiring action taken, hematoma needing surgical revision, surgical site infection (defined according to the CDC classification), wound dehiscence (skin and/or fascial, mesh removal) intraoperatively, at discharge, within 3, 6, 12, 24, and 36 month following the use of Deternia Self-Gripping Resorbable Mesh. Readmission and reoperation rate related to study mesh device and/or mesh augmented reinforcement study procedures.” [[link](https://doi.org/10.9785/cri-2023-240502)]

## 5. Data collection and management

### Item 15. Data collection, handling, management and confidentiality

* **15a** Data collection methods and study instruments: Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes, baseline, follow up and other study related data. Description of data collection methods e.g., online survey, household survey, paper based or electronic data capture. Description of study instruments (e.g., questionnaires, data collection forms) along with their reliability and validity. Reference to where data collection forms can be found, if not in the protocol
* **15b** Data handling and quality: Plans for data entry, coding, security, and storage, including any related processes to promote data quality (e.g., electronic data capture, double data entry; range checks for data values, random cross-checking of electronic data with the source documents)
* **15c** Data management procedures: Reference to where details of data management procedures e.g. plan for data collection, data entry, data quality checks, query management, and initial data analysis (IDA), can be found, if not included in the protocol itself
* **15d** Data confidentiality statement: A detailed description of process to ensure data confidentiality

**Explanation**

Providing a detailed description of data collection methods (whether prospectively planned or based on pre-existing datasets), such as online surveys or community-based surveys helps ensure that data are collected in a standardised manner. This promotes consistency across study sites and minimises variability in data collection practices.

Describe study instruments, such as questionnaires and data collection forms in the protocol, along with their reliability and validity, if known. Reference where data collection forms can be found, if not included in the protocol. If an online survey is part of the research plan, include the survey link in the protocol. This will enable reviewers to verify that the study instrument is carefully designed to capture all important variables (Item 14a) required for the study.

This item covers the data handling and quality procedures described in the protocol itself. Detailing plans for data entry, coding, security, and storage in protocols of observational study is needed for safeguarding the integrity and confidentiality (item 27c) of study data. A data entry plan could describe details on how the data will be received, how soon the data will be entered, how the data will be stored and transferred, type of data entry (single/double), approaches for training data collectors, outline methods to identify and, if possible, correct errors, and detail how to properly document these errors while retaining the original data and providing an explanation for any modifications.

The data entry plan should also detail the procedures for performing data entry quality checks.

Detailing processes in the protocol that will be incorporated during the study conduct to promote data quality during data collection, such as duplicate measurements, assessor training, and validation methods, is essential for minimising possible errors and biases. These measures help ensure that data will be accurate, reliable, and valid, enhancing the overall integrity of the study.

Additionally, it should explain how data will be handled in compliance with the data protection regulations of the country where the study is being conducted. For example, adhering to General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) requirements is mandatory in Europe [[link](https://gdpr-info.eu/),[link](https://doi.org/10.1093/bmb/ldy038)].

This item refers to a separate data management plan where these procedures may be described in more detail. Providing references to where further details of data management procedures e.g. plan for data collection, data entry, data quality checks, query management, initial data analysis (IDA) plan [[link](https://osf.io/zfjah/overview)] , and statistical analysis plan can be found, if not included in the protocol itself, allows researchers and study personnel to access comprehensive information on data management practices. Researchers should develop IDA plan in conjunction with the study objectives, design, and SAP. IDA covers all steps between data collection and formal analysis; poor IDA can lead to inappropriate methods and incorrect conclusions [[link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtcvs.2015.09.085)]. The STRATOS Initiative has developed a framework and tools to support this process [[link](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1009819)].

This ensures transparency and facilitates adherence to established protocols and guidelines. The Open Science Framework (OSF) provides useful resources for creating and registering a data management plan [[link](https://osf.io/)]. Researchers should use the data management plan template required by their funder or institution; tools include DMPTool (US), DMPonline (UK and Europe) and DMP Assistant (Canada). . For data obtained from other organisations, describe the data access request and data transfer procedures .

The inclusion of a data confidentiality statement in an observational study protocol is advisable due to the sensitive nature of the personal information collected from participants. Example of such data are details of health status, social and family history, name, email address, telephone number, or identifiable biological data etc. Ensuring data confidentiality is crucial to respecting participants' privacy and maintaining their trust in the study and its investigators.

The GDPR became binding law in the European Union in 2018, with the goal of harmonising personal data protection across EU Member States [[link](https://gdpr-info.eu/)]. Several countries outside the EU have enacted similar data protection laws, including India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023,[[link](https://doi.org/10.9785/cri-2023-240502)] and New Zealand's Privacy Act [[link](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2020/0031/latest/LMS23223.html)]. In United States the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) privacy rule was enacted to protect patients' personal health information from undue disclosure [[link](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-27979-9_15)].

While these regulations are sector-wide, they significantly impact data protection in health research [[link](https://doi.org/10.1093/bmb/ldy038)]. Therefore, researchers should consider the respective country laws when writing confidentiality statements in their protocols.

The protocol should include specific measures for collecting, securing, and maintaining personal information. These typically involve (a) securely storing data in a designated location with restricted access to authorised personnel only. (b)encrypted databases, password-protected servers, or secure cloud-based storage platforms. (c) Access to physical files or electronic databases limited to essential study staff who will be provided appropriate training on data confidentiality protocols. (d) methods to protect participants' personal identifying information (such as names, addresses, and contact details), such as replacing personal identifiers with unique codes (pseudonymisation; see Item 27c). (e) If data will be transmitted electronically how it will be encrypted to prevent unauthorised interception or access. If data sharing is planned (via email, file transfers, or online data collection platforms) as part of the study, appropriate protocols should be established to ensure that data are shared securely and in compliance with data protection regulations. This may include data anonymisation techniques, secure data transfer methods, dual authentication, and agreements with data recipients regarding data use and confidentiality.

**Examples**

> Item 15a: “Baseline data collection: Data on the Global Positioning System (GPS) position and description of the household will be collected using REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) on 4G tablets.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0283643)]

> Item 15a: “The questionnaire consists mostly of questions adapted from well-validated and widely implemented tools (online supplemental appendix A). This approach enables direct comparison of results with previously conducted surveys by incorporating questions from the Demographic and Health Surveys, WHO STEP Wise surveys for Non-communicable Disease Risk Factors (STEP) and the World Health Survey. The questionnaire underwent adjustments and validation through pre tests using behaviour coding and participant debriefing. During mock interviews, the observer of each interview pair recorded the interaction problems for each question, such as ‘major change in wording’, ‘question reading interrupted by the respondent’ or ‘additional prompt is provided’. All interviewers, interviewees and the observers participated in the debriefing. The pretest was conducted initially among peer researchers in English. Subsequently, the adjusted version was translated into Thai and tested among the target population in Thailand. This ensured the questionnaire’s suitability, comprehensibility and appropriate length for administration. The final questionnaire was then translated into the official languages of each study country (Bangla, Khmer and Thai) by professional translators or dedicated staff from partner organisations, and validated by the local research teams. When necessary, during face-to-face interviews, questions will be interpreted to dialect for participants who do not speak the official local language.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/BMJOPEN-2023-081079)]

> Item 15b: “Continuous quality control and quality assurance (QA) are achieved through automated range checks at data entry, real-time data reviews at each study site, periodic source-data verification, and a system of monthly data quality checks at central level. The queries generated by the central QA are sent to site staff who review, confirm entered data or make appropriate corrections to site EMRs and report to the central site for resolution of each query.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-019-4378-4)]

> Item 15b: “The database incorporates automated logic checks to prevent out-of-range values being entered or to feedback warnings to users when data are out of range. Each case entered into the registry is manually verified by a member of the study team and data queries are resolved with the study site. Cases with missing data or unresolved queries will be rejected to ensure only the highest quality data are retained in the registry. Source data verification and random audit will also be conducted at study sites”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1183/23120541.00081-2015)]

> Item 15c: “Data (including deposition and curation) will be managed according to our Data Management Plan (DMP2020-00062), lodged with the University of Adelaide Research Grants Office on 31 October 2018 and amended to include lipid analysis on 6 August 2020.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2020-045908)]

> Item 15d: “Data Privacy and Confidentiality To maximize data privacy and security, data from all sources are encoded and can only be matched using a key maintained in a password-protected file only accessible to approved study personnel. Study data collected from the Beiwe app are encrypted in transit and at rest, and no identifiable data are stored on participants’ devices. For complete details on all the security features of the Beiwe research platform, see [[link](https://riskcalc.org/samplesize/)]. Audio files are saved to a microSD card and returned with the recorders through registered mail. All digital data, both in raw and processed formats, are stored in a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)–compliant protected environment maintained by the University of Utah Center for High-Performance Computing.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.2196/53857)]

> Item 15d: “All information collected will be stored securely at the research site. Paper documents will be kept in locked cabinets, and electronic data will be stored on password-protected databases that can only be accessed by the research team.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2016-012131)]

### Item 16. Blinding procedures (if applicable)

* **16** Blinding procedures (if applicable): Who will be blinded to information relevant to the research question, how blinding will be maintained, and when and how it may be broken

**Explanation**

Although blinding is typically associated with interventional trials, it can also be useful in observational studies to prevent certain biases in the measurement of exposure and outcome and, in the statistical analyses of the data. In observational studies, blinding is primarily used to minimise ascertainment bias. In cohort studies, outcome assessors could be kept unaware of participants’ exposure status. Similarly, in case-control studies, those assessing exposure should be kept unaware of whether a participant is a case or a control. Furthermore, the statistical analyst could be made unaware of the most important exposure or outcome data.

SPIROS 2026 recommends including a description of the blinding procedure in the protocol, identify who will be blinded, and outline the methods for ensuring blinding and unmasking.

**Examples**

> “The microbiologists and laboratory technicians analysing the samples will be blinded as to whether the samples are from the case or control group. The statistician and orthopaedic surgeons will be blinded for the laboratory results, and these results will not be recorded in the electronic hospital patient records. Unblinding will be performed at the end of the study after all microbiological analyses are performed and documented.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/BMJOPEN-2023-082244)]

### Item 17. Potential biases

* **17** Potential biases: Description of the main potential sources of bias, their likely direction, and plans to minimise them

**Explanation**

Selection bias, information bias, and confounding are commonly present to some extent in observational research [[link](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(02)07451-2)]. It is important for an observational study researcher to anticipate and implement measures to mitigate potential biases during the study's conduct. Investigators should carefully consider potential sources of these biases during the study planning stage. For each important bias, state its likely direction, the design measures to prevent it, and the analyses planned to assess residual bias, such as negative controls, sensitivity analyses or quantitative bias analysis.

By openly acknowledging potential sources of bias, researchers can mitigate concerns about the validity and reliability of the study findings. By describing strategies to prevent or deal with these biases (e.g. propensity score matching), researchers can improve the accuracy and integrity of the study findings, thereby increasing confidence in the results.

**Examples**

> “We will also compute the proportion of survey questions for which primary informants identified secondary informants to minimise reporting bias.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13012-014-0146-4)]

> “Since the participants are pregnant women who registered for routine antenatal care, a selection bias could occur. However, since the clinic registration rate is very high this bias would be minimal.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.12688/f1000research.28226.3)]

## 6. Statistical analysis

### Item 18. Statistical analysis plan

* **18** Statistical analysis plan: Detailed description of methods for analysing and presenting primary/secondary outcomes and any additional analysis (e.g., analyses of subgroups and interactions, and sensitivity analyses). Give reference to where other details of the statistical analysis plan can be found, if not in the protocol

**Explanation**

The Statistical Analysis Plan (SAP) provides a comprehensive and detailed description of the planned analysis for a study. It usually offers a more technical and in-depth explanation of the key aspects of the analysis compared to the statistical analysis section found in the study protocol. Having a pre-specified SAP in the protocols of observational studies enhances the transparency, reproducibility, and trustworthiness of the study. It ensures that researchers do not selectively report results after analysing the data, which could introduce reporting bias [[link](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.9502)] This helps reduce the risk of data dredging or p-hacking [[link](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-020-01590-1)][[link](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-020-01590-1)].

By outlining the statistical methods and procedures in advance, researchers can minimise the risk of unconscious bias in the analysis process. This helps ensure that the analysis is conducted objectively and without influence from the results observed during the study.

SPIROS 2026 recommends that the protocol for an observational study should at least describe the variables chosen to assess the comparability of groups and specify how these variables will be reported (e.g. means, median, proportions, or other measures). Analysis plan should include clear description of primary and secondary analyses including summary measures used, methods of analysis (e.g. t-test, Chi-square test, logistic regression etc.) and how the results will be reported (e.g. odds ratios or risk ratios with 95% confidence intervals). Moreover, it should include details of adjustments for pre-defined confounders (Item 17) and methods to handling missing data (item 20) and lost to follow-up (item 19a). It should also specify when the analysis will be conducted and by whom.

Inclusion of methods for analysing subgroups and interactions in the SAP allows for a better interpretation of the results. By examining how outcomes vary across different subgroups or in response to different factors, researchers can identify potential effect modifiers and better understand the underlying mechanisms driving the observed associations. The protocol should provide details on how any planned subgroup analyses will be carried out.

Providing a reference to where other details of the SAP can be found, if not included in the protocol itself, ensures that interested parties have access to comprehensive information on the analytical methods employed. This promotes transparency and allows for detailed evaluation of the study's statistical procedures.

There are established guidelines for the content of SAP in the clinical trials [[link](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2017.18556)]. A study examined the applicability of guidelines for SAPs developed for clinical trials to observational studies and found that most of the items were directly applicable [[link](https://doi.org/10.1186/S12874-019-0879-5)]. Therefore, SAP guidelines for clinical trials can also be applied to prepare SAPs for observational studies. Additionally, a recent study provides an SAP template for observational studies that complements the STROBE statement and can be used by researchers to develop their SAP [[link](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42519-025-00504-9)].

**Examples**

> “Statistics 2.6.1 | Baseline data, ICU length of stay, and mortality: Baseline characteristics for all included patients will be presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous data, and numbers with percentages for categorical data. Data will be presented for all included patients and for patients stratified by ICU LOS quartiles. The distribution of ICU LOSs and SAPS II scores will be presented graphically, and the distribution of ICU LOSs and length of total follow‐up (from first ICU admission to 90‐days after discharge from the ICU) will be presented as medians with IQRs. Mortality 90‐day post‐ICU discharge and in‐hospital mortality will be presented for all included patients and stratified by ICU LOS quartiles as numbers with percentages. 2.6.5 | Sensitivity analyses Two sensitivity analyses will be conducted. First, the analyses will be repeated using in‐hospital mortality as the outcome measure. Patients without registered in‐hospital mortality will be excluded from these analyses. Second, the primary analyses will be conducted using only complete cases instead of the multiply imputed data sets. Two‐tailed P‐values < 0.05 will be considered statistically significant and 95% CIs used where appropriate”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1111/aas.13233)]

### Item 19. Withdrawals, loss to follow-up and replacements

* **19a** Handling of withdrawals and loss to follow up: Detailed description of the procedures to be followed when a participant ceases participation in the study prematurely or is lost to follow-up
* **19b** Replacements: Description of plans and strategies for replacing or substituting participants who withdraw, are lost to follow-up, or refuse to participate

**Explanation**

Participant withdrawal, loss to follow-up, or refusal to continue participation in observational studies can reduce the sample size and potentially affect the study's statistical power.

State the expected loss rate, retention and tracing procedures, whether data collected before withdrawal will be retained, how reasons for withdrawal will be recorded, and how participants lost will be compared with those retained (Items 18 and 20). Participants should be told at consent what will happen to their data and samples if they withdraw (Item 26a).

Plans for replacement or substitution help ensure that sample size is maintained, thereby preserving the validity and reliability of the study findings. Premature termination of participation or loss to follow-up are usually non-random and can introduce bias in cohort studies if not handled appropriately [[link](https://doi.org/10.1023/B:EJEP.0000036568.02655.f8)]. An example of this is the Healthy Worker Effect phenomenon in occupational historic cohort studies, where dropouts and incomplete follow-ups in occupational studies may be linked to workers baseline health status rather than the exposure of interest [[link](https://doi.org/10.4103/ijoem.IJOEM_53_16)].

Describing procedures about how withdrawals and lost-to follow up will be handled in the protocols of observational studies helps minimise the impact of missing data on study results and ensures that bias is minimised. Detailed procedures ensure that efforts will be made to collect as much data as possible from participants who prematurely discontinue or are lost to follow-up. This helps maintain data completeness and reduces the potential for bias in the analysis.

By documenting procedures, researchers can ensure that data collected from participants who stop participation prematurely or are lost to follow-up are handled consistently and according to established protocols.

Providing plans and methods for replacement or substitution in protocol ensures that participants who withdraw from the study are replaced in a fair and equitable manner, describe how replacements will be selected from the same sampling frame minimising any potential bias, and burden to participants.

**Examples**

> Withdrawal and handling of withdrawals “Participants (health professional or couples) may choose to withdraw at any time for any reason. Withdrawal will in no way affect participating couples' current or future medical care. If participants (health professionals/couples) withdraw, any of their data or samples that were collected will be kept, unless specifically requested to be destroyed. If samples or data have been anonymised, it may not be possible to destroy them. Participants can notify the chief investigator, in writing, of their wishes in relation to the samples and data already collected. Withdrawn samples will be discarded in a timely manner.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2018-028209)]

> “This cross-sectional descriptive observational study will analyze data from 500 subjects (250 men and 250 women) aged 18–34 without cardiovascular disease, selected by simple random sampling with replacement from the urban population of the city center of Salamanca (34,044 people aged 18–34, with 18,450 women and 15,594 men).”[[link](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2024.1322437)]

### Item 20. Handling of missing data

* **20** Handling of missing data: Detailed description of methods to handle missing data

**Explanation**

Most observational studies experience some degree of missing data, which can introduce bias depending on the pattern of "missingness".

Handling missing data effectively allows researchers to retain as much data as possible for analysis, thereby maximising statistical power and reducing the likelihood of Type II errors [[link](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.sodo.2024.01.007)] .

Although the STROBE guidelines recommend reporting missing data, a review of 82 cohort study papers found that only 35 (43%) reported the amount of missing data and 68 papers (83%) explained their methods for handling missing data in their analysis [[link](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2288-12-96)].

In line with STROBE, SPIROS 2026 also recommends developing and documenting strategies to maximise follow-up (in cohort studies), prevent missing data, and record reasons for missing data.

The protocol of observational studies should also outline how missing data will be handled, including any planned methods for imputing missing outcome data (multiple imputation methods or single imputation methods) and which variables will be used in the imputation process if applicable. Protocol should also describe any plan to do sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of study results under different methods of handling missing data. An article by Pedersen et al. provides a description of the problems and types of missing data, along with different techniques for handling them [[link](https://doi.org/10.2147/CLEP.S129785)].

**Examples**

> 2.6.2 | Missing data and multiple imputation We expect data to be not missing completely at random, and we will perform multiple imputation (with m = 25 data sets) for the missing baseline data. Multiple imputations will be generated using chained equations (also known as the fully conditional specification method) using R (R Core Team, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and the mice package. Missing SAPS II scores will be imputed using predictive mean matching with inclusion of all baseline variables with missingness <33%, ICU LOS, and 90‐day mortality. The amount of missing data will be reported. As we expect some missingness for SAPS II,10 and baseline data will be presented stratified by quartiles of this variable, patients without SAPS II scores cannot be included in the stratified quantiles in the baseline table. An additional baseline table using pooled imputed baseline data with mean number of patients across the imputed data sets rounded to nearest whole numbers will thus be presented in the supplement, to allow stratification of all patients. 2.6.5 | Sensitivity analyses Two sensitivity analyses will be conducted. First, the analyses will be repeated using in‐hospital mortality as the outcome measure. Patients without registered in‐hospital mortality will be excluded from these analyses. Second, the primary analyses will be conducted using only complete cases instead of the multiply imputed data sets. Two‐tailed P‐values < 0.05 will be considered statistically significant and 95% CIs used where appropriate”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1111/aas.13233)]

## 7. Ethics and governance

### Item 21. Follow up

* **21** Follow up: A detailed plan of follow up including schedule and methods (telephonic, house based, hospital based, digital etc.) of follow up

**Explanation**

In the protocol of a cohort study, add a detailed schedule and methods of participants follow-up information. It should outline the methods of follow-up (e.g., community-based surveys, hospital-based data collection, telephonic interviews, in-person interviews, online survey, questionnaires, or other relevant method), planned schedule for follow-up assessments, including the frequency (e.g., monthly, annually etc.) and timing of data collection points (and the window period). This schedule ensures consistency in data collection and allows researchers to track participant’s progress over time. Protocol should also specify how unscheduled visits will be handled and documented.

The protocol may include strategies for participant retention. This could involve providing incentives, maintaining regular communication, or implementing reminder systems to encourage participation in follow-up assessments.

For studies using existing data, describe how follow-up is defined in the data source (e.g., start and end of registration, death, end of the data period) and how censoring will be handled.

**Examples**

> “Two study visits are scheduled for each participant: at baseline and 1-year follow-up. Both of them include a clinical interview, physical examination (height, weight, waist circumference, blood pressure and psoriasis severity through Psoriasis Area Severity Index (PASI)), fasting blood draw and assessment of atherosclerotic disease by non-invasive vascular imaging tests (2D/3DVUS and CCTA). Participants who experience primary or secondary failure of the initial treatment may undergo an unscheduled clinical visit as part of standard clinical practice, given the observational nature of this study. The attending physician will make the decision to change treatment based on the patient’s previous medical history and clinical presentation. This visit will include a clinical interview, a physical examination and a fasting blood draw. Imaging studies will not be repeated due to the radiation exposure associated with CCTA. Training sessions and certification of all personnel involved in data collection are repeated throughout the study. Inclusion started in January 2020 with baseline visits completed for all 120 patients by the end of 2022. The remaining 1-year follow-up visits are expected to be completed by the end of 2023” [[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2023-072455)]

### Item 22. Plan of study monitoring

* **22** Plan of study monitoring: Description of plan for study monitoring and whether the monitoring will be independent from investigators and/or sponsors

**Explanation**

Study monitoring is the oversight of whether the study is conducted according to the protocol, for example protocol adherence, consent documentation, eligibility and source data verification. Monitoring should be proportionate to the study’s complexity and risk. The protocol should state who will monitor, how often, whether the monitor is independent of the investigators, sponsor and funder, whether a data and safety monitoring committee will be set up, and how findings will be reported. For low-risk studies or studies using existing data, a statement that no independent monitoring is planned, with a brief justification, is sufficient.

The monitoring plan in an observational study protocol should typically include purpose and objectives of the monitoring plan, emphasising its role in ensuring compliance to the study protocol, data quality, integrity, and participant safety. Protocol should specify whether the monitoring will be independent from investigators or sponsors and should outline procedures for reporting monitoring activities, and recommendations of relevant stakeholders, including the study team, ERB/IRB, regulatory agencies, and sponsors.

**Examples**

> “An independent study monitor (WESTAT) was hired to conduct site assessments to ensure harmonisation of study procedures across and between sites, as well as to ensure compliance with regulatory standards. Results from WESTAT’s monitoring visits are for internal purposes and will be made available to the principal investigators at each site, the study funder, and the CHAIN Network leadership and coordination teams.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2018-028454)]

### Item 23. Training plan of study staff

* **23** Training plan of study staff: Description of how investigators and data collectors will be trained to conduct the study

**Explanation**

In an observational study protocol, providing a detailed description of how investigators and surveyors will be trained to conduct the research activity is important for ensuring standardised data collection procedures, minimising variability, and maintaining the quality and integrity of the study. Training could include, training on standardised data collection methods, tools, and instruments, including interview techniques, questionnaire administration, physical examinations, and laboratory procedures.

The protocol should identify the training content, specific competencies or tasks that participants are expected to achieve through the training.

The protocol should describe the methods and formats that will be used to deliver the training, such as: In-person workshops or seminars, online training, practical exercises etc. and specifying the timing and duration of the training program, including dates, times, and locations for in-person sessions, if applicable.

For studies using existing data, this item applies to staff who extract, code or link data; otherwise, state ‘Not applicable’.

**Examples**

> “In order to ensure the reliability of the collected data, all interviewers will be trained prior to the start of the study using a variety of strategies, such as formal training sessions, practical demonstrations and role-plays, to learn how to explain the study objectives, take consent and administer the questionnaire. A training handbook will be used to ensure standardization of instructions and coherence of training norms for interviewers throughout the project.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-021-03732-6)]

### Item 24. Quality assurance and control

* **24** Quality assurance and control: Plan for quality assurance, including measures such as verifying study related processes, duplicate measurements and independent verification of source documents.

**Explanation**

Quality assurance in observational studies refers to the systematic, often periodic and independent, activities intended to verify that study procedures, documentation, and data meet an appropriate standard of quality and integrity throughout the study. It typically draws on relevant professional or disciplinary standards. Quality assurance measures can address several aspects of the study, including verification of core processes such as participant enrolment, consent procedures (item 26a), and data collection accuracy (item 15a); duplicate or independent re-measurement of a sample of data points; and independent verification of source documents against the study database. Quality assurance increasingly also covers computational reproducibility: whether the combination of the dataset and the analysis code used to produce the reported results can be re-run to reproduce the same output, for example through version control, documented code review, and a clear link between a specific code version, the dataset version it was run on, and the resulting output. Protocols should describe how quality assurance will be organised and documented, including who is responsible for carrying it out and how their independence from the study investigators and sponsor is safeguarded, the planned frequency and scope of audits or checks, and how findings, deviations and corrective actions will be recorded, for example in an audit trail or study logbook. For studies using existing data, the focus is on checks of data extraction and computational reproducibility.

**Examples**

> “Quality assurance and quality control procedures During data collection, the quality of the data will be checked weekly to identify interviewer bias. 10 % of the data will be monitored and checked monthly by an independent researcher to ensure completeness.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-021-03732-6)]

### Item 25. Ethical approval

* **25a** Ethical approval: Plan for seeking ethics approval from ethics committees/institutional review boards. If known, include details of the ethics committee(s).
* **25b** If ethics approval will not be sought, give justification.

**Explanation**

A fundamental requirement for ensuring the ethical integrity of research involving human participants involves the review and approval of the research protocol by impartial individuals unaffiliated with the research team and devoid of any conflicting interests. This review process is typically carried out by a formal Ethics review boards (ERB) or Institutional Review Board (IRB) in accordance with relevant policies.

The Declaration of Helsinki mandates that experimental studies involving human participants must be approved by a research ethics committee before they begin [[link](https://doi.org/10.1093/postmj/qgae181)]. However, ethics approval requirement for human observational studies are less clearly defined.

Currently, there is no consensus among scientific journals on the necessity of obtaining ethical committee approval for observational study designs [[link](https://doi.org/10.4103/joacp.JOACP_453_21)]. Generally, the decision to require an ethics review for retrospective studies depends on the policies of the individual ERBs/IRBs, the guidelines of the journal, and the discretion of the editor [[link](https://doi.org/10.23750/abm.v93i5.13179)].

While most observational studies do not involve interventions, they still raise ethical concerns, such as breach of confidentiality (item 27b) and respect for study participants rights.

Regulations for ethics approval of retrospective observational studies vary between countries [[link](https://doi.org/10.23750/abm.v93i5.13179)]. A survey of 16 European countries revealed considerable diversity in ethical review processes, with the identical observational study protocol requiring approval from national ethics committees, regional ethics committees, or institutional review boards in different countries [[link](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12910-019-0373-y)].

It is important that the observational study protocol explicitly states whether approval has been obtained or outlines plans to get such approval.

If a waiver from ethics review has been obtained or is planned, the protocols should specify whether the ethics review committee or a relevant authority has granted the waiver of the ethics approval requirement.

If ethical approval has been obtained, provide the reference number, date of approval, names, contact details and positioning of the ERB/s or IRBs so that it can be easily checked whether it is an existing, independent, and appropriately certified/qualified ethics committee. In a multicountry observational study, it is important to obtain approval from a local ethics committee for each country where the research is conducted.

If ethics approval will not be sought for an observational study, provide a rationale for this decision in the study protocol.

**Examples**

> “The study has been approved by the institutional review board of the University Medicine Greifswald (Ref. BB 237/20).”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2021-049944)]

> “The protocol, cohort creation and analysis plan have been approved and classified as a quality improvement initiative exempt from ethical review (Providence Health Care Research Institute and the Simon Fraser University Office of Research Ethics).”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2019-036102)]

> “The study protocol and all required documents have been submitted for review and approval to the Independent Ethics Committees of all the participating sites.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2023-073277)]

### Item 26. Consent and assent

* **26a** Consent and assent: Description of who will obtain informed consent or assent from potential study participants or authorised surrogates, and how (e.g., written informed consent, verbal consent, video/audio recording of consent procedure)
* **26b** Give reason if consent or assent will not be sought
* **26c** If consent or assent is sought, give reference to where informed consent forms and possible translations plan can be found, if not in the protocol

**Explanation**

According to the 2026 International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use Guideline for Good Clinical Practice (ICH-GCP), consent is defined as ‘A process by which a subject voluntarily confirms his or her willingness to participate in a particular trial, after having been informed of all aspects of the trial that are relevant to the subject's decision to participate’. Informed consent is documented by means of a written, signed and dated informed consent form [[link](https://database.ich.org/sites/default/files/ICH_E6%28R3%29_Step4_FinalGuideline_2025_0106.pdf)]. A typical informed consent form comprises an information sheet and a consent certificate.

Informed consent ensures participants are aware of the study details, that their participation is voluntary, and that they can withdraw anytime without consequences.

The 2002 Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences (CIOMS ) guidelines specify that consent must be signed and dated by the participant or their legal representative, as well as the person conducting the consent process. However, in cases of minimal risk research, the ethical review committee may waive some consent requirements [[link](https://cioms.ch/)]. For example, the Indian national ethical guidelines for biomedical and health research involving human participants states that “in some observational research the ethics committee may approve waiver of consent, provided mechanisms for maintaining privacy and confidentiality are justified” [[link](https://doi.org/10.4103/0253-7613.262456)].

Typically, individuals aged 18 and over can consent to participation, though this may vary by country. Assent is the affirmative agreement to participate in an activity. Adolescents aged 12 to 18 typically require parental/guardian consent and their own assent, which indicates their willingness but does not substitute for parental consent [[link](https://globalhealthtrainingcentre.tghn.org/introduction-informed-consent/)].

Most prospective observational studies with human participants will require an informed consent, and all should receive an ERB/IRB approval or exemption (if consent not required) before start of study.

In the observational study protocol, it is important to provide detailed information about the process of obtaining informed consent or assent from potential study participants or authorised surrogates. If applicable, the final version of the informed consent form must be approved by the ERB/IRB before the study begins (Item 25a). Any significant changes to the approved informed consent must be reported to the ERB/IRB. Conversely, changes required by ethics committees should be recorded as a new protocol version (Item 2).

The protocol should detail the consent process, including the type (written, oral, or online), status, experience, and training of the research team members responsible for taking consent. In cases where potential participants lack decision-making capacity, the protocol should specify procedures for obtaining proxy consent. If consent or assent not sought, reason should be provided in the protocol.

SPIROS 2026 recommends providing a consent or assent form and their applicable translations as a protocol appendix. The Global Health Network offers a free template for consent forms, specifically designed for observational studies, which can be adapted by investigators [[link](https://globalhealthtrainingcentre.tghn.org/articles/informed-consent-templates/)]. For pre-existing data, the consent method used at the original data collection should be reported.

**Examples**

> “Participants will be provided with a consent and information form when they receive the electronic survey. Students will be able to provide digital consent after reading this form by ticking a box and agreeing to proceed with the survey. The consent and information forms indicate that responses to the survey will be confidential…” “Students will be informed of the sensitive nature of survey questions in the consent and information form.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2021-051826)]

> “Waiver of consent for data collection As this is a safety initiative, consent will not be requested for the evaluation of routinely collected data of participants within the safety cohort. A waiver of consent for data collection has been granted by the Child and Adolescent Health Service Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC). A waiver of consent will ensure the safety cohort are fully representative of the patient population meaning rates of readmission can be validly compared with prior (historical) rates. Requiring consent could skew the enrolled safety cohort, resulting in either an under or overestimation of the true readmission rate and undermining the validity and scientific value of this project. Inclusion of all infants in the safety cohort will also ensure any safety issues are detected at the earliest possibility, minimising the risk to the patient population.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2019-035992)]

### Item 27. Risks, harms, anonymity and incentives

* **27a** A description of potential risks or harms to study participants
* **27b** Plans for collecting, assessing, reporting, and managing any study procedures-related adverse events (e.g. adverse events due to blood collection) and other unintended effects of study conduct (e.g., risk to breach confidential and sensitive information of participants)
* **27c** Give a statement about whether data will be anonymous, anonymised and pseudonymised, or can be directly linked to participants
* **27d** Description of any plan for giving incentives to the participants

**Explanation**

The 2002 CIOMS guidelines mention that the participation in the study should result in no intentional injury or harm for the individual [[link](https://cioms.ch/)].

Although safety concern is comparatively lower in observational studies compared to interventional trials, evaluation of harms plays a crucial role in monitoring participants condition during study and informing the management of harms.

It is also essential to remember that harm is not only medical harm e.g. adverse reaction at injection site, but harm could also include physical injury, expense and time involved in extra visits, unnecessary investigations, discomfort, psychological distress, or breach of confidentiality.

For instance, online surveys that gather sensitive or geographic information can potentially make it easier to identify respondents. Questions about high-risk behaviours, such as substance use, illegal migration, sexual orientation, or disease history, may cause discomfort for participants and pose security risks. Based on the best available evidence and any relevant experience, protocol should specify what are the anticipated (medical and non-medical) harms to the research participants and how likely and how significant are any harms effect the research participants.

Including information about potential harms in observational study protocols enables ERBs/IRBs to make informed decisions regarding approval (item 25a).

Additionally, specifying potential harms in the informed consent form is important for allowing participants to make informed decisions (Item 26a). The protocol should also describe the potential wider social harms and benefits to communities, if any. It should outline the protections that will be put in place to mitigate anticipated harms and establish a process for monitoring unknown harms or adverse events.

Whereas Item 15d describes the operational procedures for protecting data confidentiality during data collection and management, this item addresses whether data will be anonymous, anonymised or pseudonymised, and the governance of any linkage. In the observational study protocol, it should be clearly stated whether participant data will be handled according to strict confidentiality protocols. The protocol should specify whether the data and samples will be anonymous, anonymised, or pseudonymised. It should also indicate whether the data will be linked, or could potentially be linked, to other datasets and/or biological materials. If linkage is possible, the protocol must detail the protections in place to ensure data security. In the case of pseudonymised data, the protocol should identify who will have access to the coding key and describe the measures taken to protect the coding key from unauthorised access.

Definitions of anonymous, anonymised and pseudonymised (adapted from Médecins Sans Frontières Ethics Framework for Review Guidance Document, Version 6, September 2023) are provided below:

Anonymous: Data and samples are anonymous if no one, not even the researcher, can connect these to the individual who provided it. They are never labelled with personal identifiers when originally collected; no identifying information is collected from the individual, including direct identifiers such as name, address or identification number (national ID, social security, hospital patient ID, etc); neither is a coding key generated. Researchers should be aware that collection of indirect identifiers (i.e., information regarding other unique individual characteristics) might make it possible to identify an individual from a pool of subjects. For example, a study participant who is a member of a minority ethnic group might be identifiable from even a large data pool.

Anonymised data and samples are often initially coded but where the link between the study participants’ identifiers and the unique code(s) or the data between the study participants’ identifiers and the samples is subsequently deleted. Once the link has been deleted, it is no longer possible to trace the data and samples back to individual subjects through the coding key(s). Anonymisation is intended to prevent study participant re-identification. As anonymised samples and associated data are not traceable back to the study participant, it is not possible to undertake actions such as sample withdrawal, or the return of individual results, even at the study participant’s request.

Pseudonymised data and samples are labelled with at least one specific ID code and do not carry any personal identifiers. It is possible to trace the data or samples back to a given individual with the use of coding keys. Samples and/or data are indirectly traceable back to a study participant via his/her unique coding key. It is possible to undertake actions such as sample withdrawal, or the return of individual results in accordance with the study participant’s request.

By including a comprehensive description of the plan for providing incentives to participants in study protocols, researchers can ensure transparency, fairness, and adherence to their local ethical guidelines. If an incentive is given to the participants, the protocol should state the purpose of providing incentives to participants, such as to encourage recruitment, enhance retention, or compensate for wages lost due to participation in the study. Protocol should specify the types of incentives that will be offered to participants. These may include financial compensation, gift cards, vouchers, reimbursement for travel expenses, or non-monetary incentives such as educational materials, free healthcare services, or access to study-related resources. Also, the eligibility criteria for receiving incentives, such as completion of study assessments, adherence to study protocols, or reaching specific milestones in the study should be defined.

For studies using only existing data, Items 27a, 27b and 27d will often not apply; state ‘Not applicable’ with a brief reason.

**Examples**

> Item 27b: “The safety analysis will be conducted by estimating the number of participants with and without serious adverse events (SAEs) during the study. If adverse event occurs, the investigators will record it in detail in the case report form. If participants complain of side effects or abnormal reactions, or if the progress of the study threatens the safety of the participants, the investigators may decide to suspend or prematurely terminate the study for those participants, and report these to Institutional Review Board.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1097/MD.0000000000029864)]

> Item 27c: “Individual-level pseudonymised data from primary care, secondary care, laboratory test and death records will be linked and analysed in secure research environments in each UK nation.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2021-050062)]

> Item 27d: “The incentive for completing the 15-minute survey is a US $10 Amazon gift code. Once submissions are determined to be preliminarily valid after the initial data quality review and participants’ identities are confirmed, incentives are mailed to the physical address provided by baseline survey participants and emailed to follow-up survey participants. The data quality check and identity verification are typically completed within 3 days of submission, and incentives are mailed on a weekly basis. Baseline survey participants are mailed their incentives as a form of delayed gratification to deter them from attempting to take the survey multiple times.” [[link](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2017.18556)]

### Item 28. Involvement of patient/participant representatives in protocol development

* **28** Involvement of patient/participant representatives in protocol development: Patient and Public Involvement (PPI) statement including how patients or participants were involved in the planning of the study. Also give statement, if there is no plan to involve patient/participants or public in designing or any phase of executing the study

**Explanation**

Public involvement in research means that research is being carried out ‘with’ or ‘by’ members of the public rather than ‘to’, ‘about’ or ‘for’ them. Example areas for patient involvement are in defining study aims, procedure feasibility or outcome prioritisation. Patient and Public Involvement (PPI) is recommended in research projects; however, it is often inadequately described or incompletely reported [[link](https://doi.org/10.1111/hex.12935)]. A potential reason for this underreporting may be to avoid disclosing an unsuccessful PPI attempt or the absence of involvement [[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2017-020452),[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2022-063356)]. Journals such as The BMJ and BMJ Open encourage authors to include a PPI statement in the methods section of their research papers, including protocol papers.

The PPI statement in observational study protocols should provide comprehensive details, including the stage at which patients and the public were or will be first involved in the research process. It should explain how the research questions and outcome measures were developed and informed by the priorities, experiences, and preferences of patients and the public. Additionally, the statement should detail how patients and the public were involved in the study design and what will be their role in the recruitment process, and in the conduct of the study. Researchers can refer to the GRIPP2 (Guidance for Reporting Involvement of Patients and the Public) reporting guidelines [[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.j3453)], for comprehensive and standardised reporting of PPI across observational study protocols.

If researchers determine that explicit PPI strategies are not feasible, this should be justified in the protocol.

**Examples**

> “Patients or the public were not involved in the design of the study, and will not be involved in the conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2021-060536)]

> “Patient and public involvement (PPi): When the aims and objectives of this study were discussed with a local PPi group, it was agreed by the group that the possible impact of COVID-19 on the implementation of ReSPECT was important to understand. Additionally, the group members expressed that it was relevant to patients for this study to investigate which types of patients were receiving the form and if there were any subsequent effects on their healthcare. The group felt that this would help inform healthcare services on where to direct resources to ensure all appropriate patient groups have access to the ReSPECT process. At the conclusion of our data analysis, we will present our results to our PPI group and seek their guidance on dissemination of findings to patients and the public.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2021-060253)]

## 8. Dissemination

### Item 29. Communication of results and authorship

* **29a** Beyond scientific publication, describe any plans to communicate results to participants, patient groups, policymakers or the public, for example through lay summaries, community meetings or websites.
* **29b** Methods to communicate research findings (e.g., via publication (open access or closed access), reporting in results databases, or other data-sharing arrangements), including any publication restrictions
* **29c** Define authors and/or method to which to adhere to reward authorship (e.g. ICMJE recommendations)

**Explanation**

A fundamental principle in any research is the benefit of contributing to publicly available knowledge. However, many observational studies remain unpublished, with statistically non-significant results being more prone to non-publication, causing publication bias [[link](https://doi.org/10.4103/ija.IJA_142_19)]. This biased representation in the literature can lead to overestimation of benefits, underestimation of harms, and detrimental impacts on patient care and research.

To address this, protocols should include an explicit statement about how the results—whether positive, negative, or inconclusive—will be disseminated. This could be through reports, policy briefs, peer-reviewed publications, and/or oral or poster presentations. Additionally, protocols should describe procedures for submitting study results to ERBs or IRBs, including timelines and requirements for reporting.

Protocols should also include strategies for communicating findings to study participants and stakeholders, such as healthcare providers, policymakers, advocacy groups, and community organisations. Protocol should describe intentions to report results in public databases or other data-sharing platforms, promoting transparency and accessibility of findings. Furthermore, protocols should outline any agreements or arrangements for sharing study data with external researchers or collaborators, ensuring compliance with data protection regulations and ethical standards (item 33b).

Any restrictions to publish or present the study results should be explicitly described in the protocol.

Authorship on the protocol paper should be awarded to those who have made significant contributions to development of protocol. The International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) has defined authorship criteria for manuscripts submitted for publication [[link](https://doi.org/10.4103/0255-0857.136545)].

Individuals who fulfil the authorship criteria should not remain hidden (ghost authorship) and conversely, those who do not meet such criteria should not be granted authorship (honorary authorship) [[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/BMJ.D6223)]. However, a survey reported 21% honorary and ghost authorship among articles published in major medical journals in 2008 [[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.d6128)].

SPIROS 2026 recommends protocols for observational study should report all authors contributed to protocol and who will be possibly named as author in subsequent publication and criteria to reward authorship. Ten easy rules for working together to write a multi-authored paper are provided in an article by Frassl et al [[link](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1006508)].

If some protocol authors are not named as authors of subsequent publications, their role in protocol design should at least be acknowledged in the published report.

The Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE) 2023 position statement asserts that " AI tools cannot meet the requirements for authorship as they cannot take responsibility for the submitted work [[link](https://publicationethics.org/cope-position-statements/ai-author)]. SPIROS recommends against crediting artificial intelligence (AI) tools, such as large language models (LLMs), as authors (details provided at item 30).

Author can use CRediT (Contributor Roles Taxonomy) taxonomy, to describe authors roles in the research protocol and the subsequent publications [[link](https://doi.org/10.5195/jmla.2021.1294)].

**Examples**

> Item 29b: “Aggregate study results will be presented at scientific conferences and the results of the study published in one or more peer-reviewed open access journals. Study results will also be disseminated to local thyroid cancer specialists and patient support group representatives.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2017-020298)]

> Item 29b: “Deidentified datasets will be shared via the BioLINCC repository following the completion of the study.” [[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2023-073734)]

> Item 29c: “The EMBARC study group will follow the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors recommendations regarding authorship.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1183/23120541.00081-2015)]

> Item 29c: “Authors’ contributions: “Dominik Liebetrau: designed the study protocol, developed the evaluation plan, drafted the initial manuscript. Jörg Teßarek: designed the study protocol, developed the evaluation plan, critically revised the manuscript for important intellectual content. Florian Elger: designed the study protocol, developed the evaluation plan, critically revised the manuscript for important intellectual content. Sebastian Zerwes: developed the evaluation plan, critically revised the manuscript for important intellectual content. Viktoria Peters: developed the evaluation plan, critically revised the manuscript for important intellectual content. Christian Scheurig- Münkler: developed the evaluation plan, critically revised the manuscript for important intellectual content. Alexander Hyhlik-Dürr: designed the study protocol: developed the evaluation plan, critically revised the manuscript for important intellectual content. Final approval of the version to be published was given by all authors. DL and AD take responsibility for the work and controlled the decision to publish. The corresponding author attests that all listed authors meet the authorship criteria and that no others meeting the criteria have been omitted.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1186/s42155-023-00404-8)]

## 9. Others

### Item 30. Use of AI-assisted technology

* **30a** Use of Artificial Intelligence (AI) assisted technology: Disclose the use of artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted technologies in the production of the protocol, or indicate whether there are plans to use AI-assisted technologies in the conduct of the study or in the production of manuscripts or other study-related outputs.
* **30b** Provide the name(s) and version(s) of the generative AI tool(s) used or planned, their purpose, and whether the authors reviewed and edited the output.

**Explanation**

The use of generative artificial intelligence (AI) tools such Large Language Models (LLM) in writing research publications is expanding rapidly. A large language model is a type of artificial intelligence designed to understand, generate, and manipulate human language. These models are trained on large amounts of text data and use complex algorithms to learn patterns and structures in language. Students and researchers have been increasingly turning to AI tools to help make their academic writing easier and more efficient [[link](https://doi.org/10.1590/1806-9282.20230560)].

However, there are concerns around plagiarism, information inaccuracy and ethics in using AI tools in research paper writing [[link](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13054-023-04380-2),[link](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10439-023-03140-1)]. A recent systematic review of 24 studies showed six core areas where AI supports academic writing and research: 1) facilitating idea generation and research design, 2) improving content and organisation, 3) aiding in literature review and synthesis, 4) enhancing data management and analysis, 5) supporting editing, review, and publishing, and 6) assisting with communication, outreach, and ethical compliance [[link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmpbup.2024.100145)].

Many publishers like Springer Nature and Elsevier have introduced a policy requiring authors to disclose the use of AI and AI-assisted technologies in their writing [[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-077192)].

SPIROS also recommend that the authors should disclose in their protocols whether they used AI-assisted technologies in the production of the protocol or if there are plans to use AI-assisted technologies in the data analysis or production of subsequent manuscript or study reports. Authors must review and edit the output resulting from the use of generative AI tools.

In line with the BMJ recommendations on the AI use,[[link](https://authors.bmj.com/policies/ai-use/)]. SPIROS recommend that the disclosure should include the name of the AI tool(s) used, reason of using the AI technology, how the AI technology was used, and any relevant statements regarding the review and editing of content created by AI-assisted technologies to ensure accuracy, completeness, no plagiarism, and alignment with research objectives. Routine language tools, such as spelling and grammar checkers, do not need to be disclosed.

There are recent debates about authorship policy on AI tools such as ChatGPT [[link](https://doi.org/10.3348/kjr.2023.0383),[link](https://doi.org/10.53761/1.20.5.01)]. leading academic journals, such as Nature, Science, and JAMA, have recently stated editorial policies that prohibit the inclusion of these nonhuman technologies as authors (Item 29c). ICMJE recommends use of AI for writing assistance should be reported in the acknowledgment section not as an author.

**Examples**

> Declaration of generative AI and AI-assisted technologies in the writing process: We acknowledge the use of AI tools in the preparation of this manuscript. Specifically, we used the [AI Tool Name] (version [X.X], developed by [developer]) to assist with [specific tasks, e.g., drafting sections of the protocol, language editing]. The authors take full responsibility for the integrity and accuracy of the content generated by these tools.

### Item 31. References

* **31** References: A complete list of references cited in protocol

**Explanation**

It is essential to provide in-text citations and references for all published and unpublished work mentioned in the study protocol. Researchers typically provide evidence for a literature review in the introduction and methods sections to justify the study's rationale and describe what is already known about the topic. Clear references in the protocol help reviewers verify cited references and ensure there is no plagiarism. The citation and reference style (e.g., APA, MLA, Vancouver) must be consistent throughout the reference list and should include at least the author's name, year of publication, title, and should include a DOI or, where unavailable, a persistent URL.

**Examples**

> “References Prasad N, Sharples KJ, Murdoch DR, et al. Community prevalence of fever and relationship with malaria among infants and children in low-resource areas. Am J Trop Med Hyg 2015;93:178–80. Fink G, D'Acremont V, Leslie HH, et al. Antibiotic exposure among children younger than 5 years in low-income and middle-income countries: a cross-sectional study of nationally representative facility-based and household-based surveys. Lancet Infect Dis 2020;20:179–87. ”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/BMJOPEN-2020-045826)]

### Item 32. Funding

* **32** Funding: Source of any funding for the study and the role of the funders for the study

**Explanation**

In observational study protocols, it is essential to disclose both financial and non-financial support to ensure transparency and identify potential biases or competing interests. Such disclosure helps assess the study's feasibility and the risk of bias. Item 4 identifies sponsors and funders and their roles, Item 32 lists funding sources and grant details, and Item 5 covers investigators’ competing interests; cross-referencing between these items is acceptable.

Protocols should specify the sources of financial and non-financial support, full names of commercial companies that funded the study or authors, URLs to sponsors websites, the types of support provided (such as funds, equipment, drugs, or services), initials of authors who received each award, the time periods of support (if time-bound), any vested interests that funders might have, and the specific grant number. If the study is unfunded at the initial protocol stage, proposed sources of support should be listed and updated as funders are confirmed.

Also state whether any sponsors or funders (other than the named authors) played any role in the study design, or if they will have a role in the data collection and analysis, decision to publish, and, preparation of the manuscript.

Some guidelines recommend including the monetary amounts granted by each funder, the mechanisms of financial support, and the specific recipients of the funds. Detailed disclosure allows ERBs or IRBs (item 25a) to assess whether the fundings are reasonable, ensuring transparency and helping to identify potential COI (Item 5).

**Examples**

> “DECIPHER is hosted by EMBL-EBI and funding for the DECIPHER project was provided by the Wellcome Trust (grant number WT223718/Z/21/Z). For the purpose of open access, the author has applied a CC BY public copyright licence to any Author Accepted Manuscript version arising from this submission. KL and GenROC are supported by the National Institute for Health and Care Research Doctoral Research Fellowship 302303: the views expressed are those of the author(s) and not necessarily those of the NIHR or the Department of Health and Social Care.” [[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2024-085237)]

### Item 33. Registration and data sharing

* **33a** Registration of observational study: Study identifier and registry name. If not yet registered, name of intended registry.
* **33b** Data sharing: Plans, if any, for granting public access to the (1) full protocol and amendments, (2) participant-level data set, (3) statistical analysis plan, (4) statistical codes and other study material (e.g., case report forms, study questionnaires and informed consent forms). Give reference to where these documents can be found, if not included as annexure in the protocol

**Explanation**

Registration of protocols of interventional studies has long been the standard, and there has been substantial discussion about the registration of observational research [[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-076123),[link](https://doi.org/10.1136/BMJ.C950),[link](https://doi.org/10.1503/CMAJ.092252)]. Registration of observational study protocols is essential to enhance the transparency, reliability, and accountability of such research. Additionally, it reduces unnecessary duplication of studies, assists potential participants in finding ongoing research, and prevents selective reporting of results.

The study design, hypothesis, methods, and analysis of observational study protocols can be registered on a suitable trial registry (e.g. ClinicalTrials.gov or Clinical Trials Registry – India (CTRI)) and openly accessible platforms like the OSF [[link](https://osf.io/sgphn)], protocols.io, bio-protocol, Zenodo, Figshare and AsPredicted [[link](https://aspredicted.org/)]. Alternatively, a full study protocol can be published in an academic journal such as BMJ Open and JMIR Research Protocols before data collection. Another option is to submit the protocol as a ‘Registered Report’, where it undergoes peer review and is pre-accepted prior to data analysis [[link](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-021-01193-7)].

SPIROS 2026 recommends registration of observational study protocols before the start of data collection. If registered, report the name of the registry, date of registration, and study identifiers in the protocol.

If the study is not yet registered, specify the intended registry and update the protocol upon registration. When multiple registrations are required to meet local regulations, list each identifier clearly in the protocol and each registry. Investigators should indicate whether the protocol has been published in an academic journal, submitted as a registered report, or if there are plans to do so. The process of registering an observational study in CTRI and OSF is well described in the article by Mondal et al [[link](https://doi.org/10.4103/ijves.ijves_87_21)].

SPIROS 2026 supports open science and recommends that every step of a research project, from inception to dissemination, should be publicly available and accessible via available data repositories when possible [[link](https://doi.org/10.1080/21642850.2021.2012474)].

In the observational study protocol, it is important to outline plans for data sharing, including access to the full protocol and its amendments, study documents, and datasets.

The protocol should describe intentions for granting public access to the participant-level dataset, including data collected during the study. The dogma is to be as open as possible while remaining as closed as necessary. If some data cannot be shared openly due to confidentiality concerns researchers are encouraged to flag the issues and provide a clear rationale for not sharing.

The planned methods of statistical analysis should be fully described in the protocol. Protocol should also describe if there is a separate detailed statistical analysis plan (SAP) (item 18). As with the protocol time-stamped SAP and amendments should be publicly available in suitable repositories.

Protocol should explain plans for sharing statistical codes and other study materials, such as case report forms, study questionnaires, and informed consent forms and applicable translations if not available as annexure in the protocol.

The protocol should specify where these materials will be made available, such as a dedicated website, data repository, or supplementary materials section of a publication. Many repositories such as OSF now provide free digital object identifiers (doi), a unique and permanent link to the data and materials, that can be cited in research reports, journal articles, and other forms of research dissemination [[link](https://www.nature.com/sdata/policies/repositories)].

**Examples**

> Item 33a: “Name of Primary Registry: “Biomarkers of Airway Disease, Barrett's and Underdiagnosed Reflux Noninvasively (BADBURN)”. Trial Identifying Number: NCT05216133. Date of Registration: January 31, 2022.”[[link](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-024-03294-9)]

> Item 33b: Data sharing “Coded data that contain no identifiers will be handled and analysed with the support of a private, password-protected github repository. The final dataset will include demographic, health history, psychological, and physiological (e.g., immunological) data. An important priority when considering sharing of these data is participant confidentiality, particularly considering the stigma and personal social risk associated with HIV+ status in South Africa. If the risk to participants can be eliminated, then: The final dataset will be stripped of individual identifiers. The final, de-identified dataset will be assessed for opportunities to deductively identify individual participants and additional stripping performed as necessary. The data and associated documentation will be made available to qualified users through a data sharing platform approved by the institutional HREC. A data-sharing agreement will provide for: (1) a commitment to using the data only for research purposes and not to identify any individual participant; and (2) a commitment to securing the data using appropriate computer technology.
